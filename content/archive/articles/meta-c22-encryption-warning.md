Meta says it would have to "consider" pulling its encrypted services out of Canada if the federal government's lawful-access bill passes without further changes.

Bill C-22 would require tech companies to adapt their systems so they can hand over information to investigators who have a warrant. It is now being studied by the Senate's national security committee, where Meta representatives testified Monday evening.

"Ultimately, the Canadian government would be enforcing and imposing a law that it is not technically feasible to comply with and still offer secure, end-to-end encrypted services to Canadians," Robyn Greene, Meta's director of privacy and public policy, told senators, CBC News reported.

Asked whether the company would pull services, Rachel Curran, Meta's head of public policy for Canada, said Meta would have to "review all of our options," and that it is something the company would have to "consider." Meta owns Facebook, Instagram and the encrypted messaging app WhatsApp.

## What the bill does

The bill would also give the public safety minister power to issue confidential orders requiring electronic service providers to build surveillance and monitoring capabilities into their systems. Those orders would first be reviewed by the intelligence commissioner, an independent quasi-judicial official.

Before the bill went to the Senate in June, Public Safety Minister Gary Anandasangaree introduced amendments limiting when companies would have to decrypt information and cutting the time they must keep metadata to six months from up to a year. Curran told senators those changes protect "just the core and not the outside layers" of encryption.

## Other tech firms and the privacy watchdog

Signal has repeatedly said it would leave Canada if the bill passes. "If we are forced to choose between betraying our users and leaving Canada, we will not compromise the security of Canadians," Signal vice-president Udbhav Tiwari told the committee. Apple said earlier this month that the amendments aren't enough.

Privacy Commissioner Philippe Dufresne has urged the Senate to amend the bill further. It would lower the legal threshold for police and intelligence agencies to get basic subscriber information, such as names, addresses, phone numbers and email addresses, from "reasonable grounds to believe" to "reasonable grounds to suspect" that an offence has been or will be committed.

The government says the bill is needed so law enforcement has the legal tools to prevent, investigate and respond to modern crime.
